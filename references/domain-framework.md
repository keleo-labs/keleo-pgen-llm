## **Resource Assessment Framework**

The following rubric was developed to provide a way to classify a specific resource against this modernized Resource Assessment Framework, combining traditional resource assessment with a continuous, co-creative mindset.

### **The 4 Perspectives of Modern Enterprise Architecture**

Below are the key themes, categorised into four major perspectives, which can be used to classify and then assess specific documentation and other resources.

**1\. Business Perspective (Value & Outcome Orientation)**

This perspective aligns with organisational goals, realisation of business value, and addressing stakeholder needs. Modern EA starts with the business, not the technology. The goal is to map how the enterprise delivers value and ensure technical decisions support that flow.

* **Value Streams & Capabilities:** Architecture is organized around business capabilities and customer journeys. Technology is viewed merely as an enabler for these capabilities. Documentation covers addressed challenges, opportunities, improved growth potential, and employee satisfaction/retention.  
* **Strategic Themes to Execution:** You maintain strategic fit by ensuring every architectural initiative maps back to a clearly defined business objective or outcome. If it doesn't move the needle on a strategic theme, it isn't prioritized. Financial models and calculators, such as Return on Investment (ROI) and Total Cost of Ownership (TCO), support this strategy.  
* **Stakeholder Alignment & Risk:** Profiles of personas (individuals seeking value or having an interest in the solution), providing an understanding of need and motivation, and used to align proposed solutions with target audiences and potential value. Resources cover reducing business risks, improving IT security, reducing legal liabilities, and improving compliance.

**2\. Technology Perspective (Continuous & Adaptive Design)**

This perspective covers the IT systems, architectures, and artifacts required to implement, operate, and scale a solution. The environment will change faster than you can build a comprehensive future-state blueprint.

* **Minimal Viable Architecture (MVA) & Design:** Define just enough architecture to get started. You provide a structural foundation that allows development to proceed, fully expecting to adapt it based on feedback. This includes abstract or generic solution architectures (e.g., highly available DR), architectures for common business challenges (e.g., distributed retail IT), and technical blueprints to build specific solutions.  
* **The Architectural Runway:** Build technical infrastructure only slightly ahead of the business features that need it. This prevents over-engineering for a future that may never happen.  
* **Integration & Ecosystem:** API descriptions, integration patterns, named/descriptive roles of external systems, detailed integration instructions, integration use-cases, and external dependencies. Documentation must include functional descriptions, installation and configuration steps, hardware requirements, sizing guidance based on usage, and supported workloads.  
* **Continuous Refactoring & Deployment:** Treat the architecture itself as a living product that requires ongoing maintenance, refactoring, and pruning of technical debt. Validation incorporates reference code (GitHub), deployment artifacts (local/suitable environments), customisation guides (swapping models/connecting data), evaluation metrics (benchmarks/audits), interactive video demos, modular solution patterns, and fully validated production-ready patterns. Lifecycle management provides migration guidance for moving workloads from other systems to the target system.

**3\. People Perspective (Stakeholder Co-Creation & Perspectives)**

This perspective focuses on the organisational patterns, team structures, and skill sets necessary to support and sustain a solution. A system cannot be understood from a single viewpoint. The modern framework demands that EA is a highly collaborative discipline.

* **Targeted Viewpoints & Team Design:** You must speak the language of your audience. C-level executives need to see business capabilities and ROI; Agile teams need to see APIs and data models. The underlying truth is the same, but the "viewpoint" shifts. This requires defined team compositions, headcount requirements, required competencies, specific team responsibilities, team roles, business objectives, and the services the team provides and requires.  
* **Human-Centered Design:** Treat the business units and development teams as your "customers." Architects embed themselves with Agile teams to co-create solutions rather than handing down edicts.  
* **Roles, Competencies, & Change Management:** Identification of stakeholders, role templates, specific skills, expected competency/experience levels, and levels of responsibility (for example, the RACI model: Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed). It outlines strategies for adapting to or building new teams, business rationale for the change, and guidance on navigating common organisational challenges during transition.

**4\. Process Perspective (Lean & Enabling Governance)**

This perspective maps out the workflows, operational procedures, and behavioral realisation of value from a solution. Governance in this generalized framework is not about saying "no"; it is about creating safe boundaries within which teams can move fast.

* **Intentional Architecture vs. Emergent Design:** The enterprise architect provides the "Intentional Architecture" (the strategic guardrails, cross-cutting concerns, and integration standards). Within those guardrails, development teams have the autonomy to use "Emergent Design" (figuring out the best local implementation).  
* **Systems Thinking over Local Optimization:** EA ensures that individual teams aren't building localized solutions that break the broader enterprise system. Governance focuses on integration points, data sharing, and cross-team dependencies.  
* **Workflows & Value Realisation:** Outlines step-by-step technical documentation for specific tasks, user guides, and outline processes/workflows. Success is mapped via use-case or user-journey scenarios demonstrating how a particular role or persona obtains value from the system.  
* **Strategy & Industry Alignment:** Governed by guiding principles, transition/change plans, and strategies for adoption, scaling use, and growing value. Embraces references to established industry terms (e.g., DevOps, GitOps, Platform Engineering), including descriptive context, expectations, and detailed definitions.

### **Operating the Generalized Framework (The Cycle)**

Instead of a linear step-by-step process, this modern framework operates as a continuous, iterative loop:

1. **Sense & Strategize:** Understand the business drivers. What are the strategic themes? (Using systems thinking to see the big picture).  
2. **Translate & Map:** Map these themes to value streams and business capabilities. Identify the architectural gaps.  
3. **Co-Create the Runway:** Work with stakeholders and delivery teams to define the Minimal Viable Architecture. Establish the guardrails (Intentional Architecture).  
4. **Deliver & Emerge:** Delivery teams build and release value, utilizing emergent design for local decisions.  
5. **Feedback & Refactor:** Measure the business outcomes. Refactor the architecture based on real-world feedback and changing conditions. Repeat.
